What we work on

Bone, muscle, and fat: body composition and physical function

뼈와 근육이 줄고 지방이 느는 변화는 함께 옵니다. 체중만으로는 그 변화를 모두 반영하지 못합니다.

Bone loss, muscle loss, and fat gain do not arrive separately. When all three converge — a condition described in the literature as osteosarcopenic obesity — neither body weight nor BMI reveals it. People who look unremarkable on a scale can be the most functionally vulnerable.

Capturing all three compartments at once remains difficult with the population data currently available in Korea, where bone density has been assessed in only a limited number of survey cycles. We therefore begin from the muscle–fat axis, in conditions such as dynapenic abdominal obesity, and work toward study designs that bring bone back into the picture. Closing that gap is one of the longer-term aims of the lab.

  • bone mineral density

  • phase angle

Precision Nutrition: Metabolic phenotype and interindividual variability

같은 식사가 모두에게 같게 작용하지는 않습니다. 흡수와 대사의 개인차를 설명하는 지표를 찾아, 어떤 사람에게 어떤 영양 중재가 맞는지를 구분하려 합니다.

The same meal does not act the same way in every body. We look for measurable markers that explain differences in absorption and metabolic response, and ask which nutritional interventions work for whom.

Rather than beginning with genomics, we start from clinically measurable phenotypes, aiming for stratification criteria that can actually be used in practice.

  • precision nutrition
  • nutritional assessment
  • absorption and utilisation
  • treatment effect heterogeneity

Nutritional vulnerability and its social context

누가 왜 영양적으로 취약해지는지를 개인 요인과 사회적 조건을 함께 넣어 분석합니다.

Cancer survivors, older adults, and socially isolated people do not arrive at the same outcome from the same advice. We analyse who becomes nutritionally vulnerable and why, holding individual and socioeconomic factors in the same model.

Translating those findings into the language of policy and community programmes is part of the work, not an afterthought.

  • cancer survivorship
  • healthy ageing
  • social-ecological model
  • health equity
  • public health nutrition policy

IASGO 2026 · KNHANES 2008–2024

Adequate intake, inadequate status: anemia and underweight in gastrointestinal cancer survivors

We compared gastric, colorectal, and other cancer survivors across fifteen survey years. Iron intake adequacy was essentially identical across the three groups, yet anemia and underweight clustered sharply among gastric cancer survivors and showed no attenuation even ten years after diagnosis. The dissociation between what is eaten and what the body retains points to a problem dietary modification alone is unlikely to correct, and argues for routine hematologic monitoring with absorption-targeted intervention.

  • 2.20prevalence ratio, anemia
  • 4.28prevalence ratio, underweight

ICOMES 2026 · KNHANES 2022–2024

Multilevel risk factors for dynapenic abdominal obesity among Korean older adults

Applying the Social-Ecological Model to 4,058 adults aged 65 and older, we entered intrapersonal, interpersonal, community, and policy factors in sequence. Nearly all explanatory gain came from the intrapersonal level, driven by physical inactivity and sedentary time. That result sharpens the next question rather than closing it: if the action is at the individual level, what distinguishes one individual from another?

  • 4,058older adults
  • 7.6%weighted prevalence
  • 1.49aOR, physical inactivity
  • 16.1%prevalence at age 80+

Hyehyung Shin

Ph.D., MPH · Assistant Professor

영양학과 보건정책을 함께 공부했습니다. 정책기관과 기업을 거쳐 대학으로 돌아오는 동안 관심의 축은 바뀌지 않았습니다. 영양이 몸에 실제로 남기는 결과, 그리고 그것이 사람마다 달라지는 이유입니다.

Trained in both nutritional science and health policy. Ph.D. from Florida State University on body composition and physical performance in postmenopausal women; MPH in health policy and management from Seoul National University.

Worked on chronic disease management and national nutrition policy at the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service and the Korea Health Industry Development Institute, then spent a decade at Samsung Life researching ageing and chronic disease prevention. Across policy, industry, and now the university, one question has stayed constant: what nutrition actually leaves behind in the body, and why that differs from one person to the next.
